      How this works

      Slopes from a 30 m surface model (Copernicus GLO-30), the finest open terrain data for this area: Canada's lidar hasn't been flown here. Zoomed out, the map shows a 90 m summary that keeps each 90 m cell's average 30 m steepness; the route check always uses 30 m. Parks Canada doesn't publish its avalanche path atlas as open data, so mapped paths aren't shown for Rogers Pass. Glacier outlines are from the BC Freshwater Atlas (provincial mapping that is decades old in places); the glaciers have shrunk since, so expect less ice than shown, especially at the snouts. It smooths short steep rolls and gully walls, so real angles can be steeper, and forest edges can show as false steep patches. Names from the Canadian Geographical Names Database; summit heights are left off the labels because the 30 m model clips peaks by tens of metres (hover still shows the model height). A planning aid, not a substitute for measuring slopes on the ground.

        How this works

        Every 10 m of the route is checked against the terrain model for: the slope underfoot (30° and up is avalanche terrain, 35° and up is steep); slopes of 30–60° above the route that face it, within 1 km and at least 30 m higher, steep enough to reach it (an angle of 22° or more up from the route to the start zone, the "alpha angle" used to screen avalanche runouts; 28° or more from a 35°+ slope counts as high); and cliffs or very steep drops within 40 m below. "Today" marks sections whose terrain matches the Terrain to watch filter, when that view is on. This reads the terrain only. It knows nothing about the snowpack, and can miss small features or flag paths that are sheltered in reality: a screening tool for planning, not a substitute for the bulletin, training and judgement on the day.

        Trips for today

        How this works

        Parks Canada's rated trips in Glacier and Mount Revelstoke national parks, graded by their official avalanche terrain rating (ATES): easy is Simple terrain, medium is Challenging, epic is Complex. A trip is listed for today only when its terrain is within strict limits for today's danger rating: at Low, any class; at Moderate, Simple or Challenging; at Considerable, Simple only; at High or Extreme, nothing. Complex trips are also held back whenever the bulletin lists a persistent or deep persistent slab problem. With no rating for today (early season, or a forecast that hasn't been updated), nothing is listed.

        —

        How this works

        Within these limits is not the same as safe. The rating describes a trip's terrain, not today's snowpack; conditions vary across a trip and through the day. Before you go: read the full forecast, check the day's Rogers Pass winter restricted areas (closed areas change daily for highway avalanche control) and get a Glacier National Park winter permit, carry rescue gear and know how to use it. The trips have no mapped tracks here, so they can't be drawn on the map or ranked against each other; the route check on the slope map above works on any GPX you upload.

                      How this works

                      Slopes from the 30 m Copernicus GLO-30 surface model: Tirol's 5 m lidar model couldn't be downloaded from here. Zoomed out, the map shows a 90 m summary that keeps each cell's average 30 m steepness; the route check always uses 30 m. It smooths short steep rolls and gully walls, so real angles can be steeper, and forest edges can show as false steep patches. Glacier outlines from the Austrian glacier inventories (Austrian Academy of Sciences, on PANGAEA): Stubai and Ötztal mapped 2017–18, Zillertal (Tux) 2006, so the Tux ice has shrunk since; glaciers on the Italian side of the main ridge aren't included. Names from GeoNames; a summit name is placed only where the terrain model has a top at its published height, so a few are left off and those tops show their model height. A planning aid, not a substitute for measuring slopes on the ground.

                      How this works

                      Select a section to zoom the map to it. Most avalanche accidents are triggered by the victim or someone in their group, so the slope underfoot always counts. A slope above counts in full only when today’s data gives it a reason to come down on you: considerable or higher danger at its elevation, a persistent slab problem with the start zone within 300 m (these can be triggered from a distance), a wet-snow problem, 20 cm or more of new snow forecast, a wind-loaded start zone, or warm sun on it when you pass. Otherwise it is shown as exposure. Other parties above you can still trigger it, so cross one at a time and don’t stop underneath. Each Show box matches a group in this table (a section on a slope you could trigger counts as “On the slope” even when there is also a slope above). The Show options apply to this table, the map, the profile, Previous / Next on the map and the watch file.

                        Timing uses your start time (local time at the map) and pace to estimate when you reach each section (Munter method: an hour per 4 km plus 400 m climbed at a steady pace, faster downhill), then checks where the sun is and the day's forecast warmth.                         How this works

                        Every 10 m of the route is checked against the terrain model for: the slope underfoot (30° and up is avalanche terrain, 35° and up is steep); slopes of 30–60° above the route that face it, within 1 km and at least 30 m higher, steep enough to reach it (an angle of 22° or more up from the route to the start zone, the "alpha angle" used to screen avalanche runouts; 28° or more from a 35°+ slope counts as high); and cliffs or very steep drops within 40 m below. "Today" marks sections whose terrain matches the Terrain to watch filter, when that view is on. This reads the terrain only. It knows nothing about the snowpack, and can miss small features or flag paths that are sheltered in reality: a screening tool for planning, not a substitute for the bulletin, training and judgement on the day.
