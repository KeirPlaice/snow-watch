      How this works

      Slopes from a 30 m surface model (Copernicus GLO-30), the finest open terrain data for this area: Canada's lidar hasn't been flown here. Zoomed out, the map shows a 90 m summary that keeps each 90 m cell's average 30 m steepness; the route check always uses 30 m. Parks Canada doesn't publish its avalanche path atlas as open data, so mapped paths aren't shown for Rogers Pass. Glacier outlines are from the BC Freshwater Atlas (provincial mapping that is decades old in places); the glaciers have shrunk since, so expect less ice than shown, especially at the snouts. It smooths short steep rolls and gully walls, so real angles can be steeper, and forest edges can show as false steep patches. Names from the Canadian Geographical Names Database; summit heights are left off the labels because the 30 m model clips peaks by tens of metres (hover still shows the model height). A planning aid, not a substitute for measuring slopes on the ground.

        How this works

        Every 10 m of the route is checked against the terrain model for: the slope underfoot (30° and up is avalanche terrain, 35° and up is steep); slopes of 30–60° above the route that face it, within 1 km and at least 30 m higher, steep enough to reach it (an angle of 22° or more up from the route to the start zone, the "alpha angle" used to screen avalanche runouts; 28° or more from a 35°+ slope counts as high); and cliffs or very steep drops within 40 m below. "Today" marks sections whose terrain matches the Terrain to watch filter, when that view is on. This reads the terrain only. It knows nothing about the snowpack, and can miss small features or flag paths that are sheltered in reality: a screening tool for planning, not a substitute for the bulletin, training and judgement on the day.

            How this works

            Slopes at 10 m. About half the map is lidar: the BC government's LidarBC surveys (2017–2025) and NRCan's HRDEM, covering the Rossland Range and Red Mountain, Nelson and Whitewater, and Kootenay Pass. The rest (mostly the far west and north) is the 30 m Copernicus GLO-30 surface model, which reads softer and can show forest edges and cut blocks as false steep patches; the two are blended over about 100 m. Faint straight lines can show where lidar surveys from different years meet. Zoomed out, the map shows a 90 m summary of the 10 m steepness; the route check uses 10 m. Glacier outlines (Kokanee, Woodbury) are from the BC Freshwater Atlas, decades-old provincial mapping, so today's ice is smaller. The weather stations are the highway avalanche program's: Stagleap and Southridge are on ridges, the others on passes. Names and provincial park outlines from the Canadian Geographical Names Database; peaks are pinned to the earlier 30 m model's summits, and none are named south of the US border. A planning aid, not a substitute for measuring slopes on the ground.
